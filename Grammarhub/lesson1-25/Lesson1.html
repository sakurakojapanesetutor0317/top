Start with noun sentences

These patterns introduce your name, nationality, occupation, or affiliation. は marks the topic and is pronounced wa. The particle か makes a polite question. Japanese does not need a separate verb equivalent to English “am / is / are” in these sentences.

わたしは アンです。
わたしは 学生がくせいです。

I am Ann.
I am a student.

先生せんせいじゃありません。
会社員かいしゃいんです。

I am not a teacher.
I work for a company.

A：学生がくせいですか。
B：はい、学生がくせいです。

A: Are you a student?
B: Yes, I am.

A：先生せんせいですか。
B：いいえ、先生せんせいじゃありません。

A: Are you a teacher?
B: No, I am not.

A：わたしは 学生がくせいです。
B：わたしも 学生がくせいです。

A: I am a student.
B: I am a student too.

さくら大学だいがくの 学生がくせいです。

I am a student at Sakura University. — の connects the affiliation to the person’s role.

Useful connection details

も replaces topic は in the “also” pattern: わたしも学生がくせいです, not わたしはも学生がくせいです.

じゃありません is a common polite negative; ではありません is a fuller, often more formal version. The conversational じゃないです is also common.

の connects nouns, but does not always mean possession: 大学だいがくの学生がくせい means a student affiliated with that university. It is not an all-purpose connector after every Japanese word.

02

Natural Conversation

Subjects, names, and forms of address

わたし is useful — but you do not need it in every sentence

Use わたし when introducing yourself, identifying who you mean, or making a contrast. Once it is clear that you are talking about yourself, Japanese often leaves it out.

Japanese does use わたし. The point is not to repeat it automatically every time English uses “I.” The same applies to other topics and subjects when the context makes them clear.

A natural short introduction

わたしは アンです。
学生がくせいです。
さくら大学だいがくの 学生がくせいです。

I am Ann. I am a student at Sakura University. — introduce the topic, then leave it understood.

The context supplies “I”

A：お仕事しごとは？
B：日本語にほんごの 先生せんせいです。

A: What do you do?
B: I am a Japanese teacher. — no わたしは is needed here.

あなた is not an automatic translation of “you”

In English, “you” is ordinary in many questions. Japanese more often uses the person’s name + さん, an appropriate role such as 先生せんせい, or no explicit subject when the listener is obvious.

あなた is not forbidden, but repeated use can sound distant, pointed, or unnatural depending on the context. It has other uses, including in general writing or some close relationships. Beginners should start with names, titles, and omission.

Prefer a name or omission

アンさんは 学生がくせいですか。
学生がくせいですか。

Are you a student, Ann?
Are you a student? — if it is clear who you are asking.

Use the appropriate title

先生せんせいは 日本人にほんじんですか。

Are you Japanese? — addressing a teacher with the title 先生せんせい.

Names and forms of address

〜さん: a safe general choice for many people; it is not limited to one gender.
先生せんせい: a role used to address teachers and some other professionals; 名前なまえ + 先生せんせい is also used.
〜ちゃん: affectionate or familiar; common with children and close relationships.
〜くん: common for boys and in certain peer or senior-to-junior relationships; usage depends on the setting.

Do not add さん to your own name: ✓ アンです。 / ✗ アンさんです。 when introducing yourself. Do not choose ちゃん or くん for a new acquaintance just because they are young.

03

How We Say “I”

Self-reference as a social choice

More than different translations of “I”

People have several ways to refer to themselves. A choice can communicate formality, closeness, regional identity, group membership, or how someone wants to present themselves. In that sense, it can be part of self-expression.

It is not a fixed label: the same person may use different forms at work, with friends, and with family. You cannot reliably infer someone’s gender or personality from one self-reference alone.

Self-reference Typical impression and context
わたし A broadly useful starting point. Common in polite situations across genders; also common in casual speech, especially among women. It does not have exactly the same tone in every relationship.
わたくし A more formal, often humble-sounding choice in business, speeches, or formal introductions; used across genders.
ぼく Often associated with boys and men, with a generally less rough impression than おれ. Some other speakers choose it too; it is not a rule that only men may use it.
おれ Strongly informal and often associated with men. Common among close friends for some speakers; inappropriate as a default with customers or unfamiliar people.
うち Used by some speakers, especially in certain regions and communities. It can sound natural within that group, but is not a universal beginner substitute for わたし.
自分じぶん Used for self-reference in some workplaces, sports communities, and individual styles. In other constructions it means “oneself”; its role depends on the sentence.

For Lesson 1, start with わたし

You do not need to choose a special self-reference immediately. わたし is a practical starting point for polite beginner conversation. As you hear real speakers, notice who uses each form, with whom, and in which setting.

Other options include regional forms, a person’s own name, and other community-specific choices. Avoid choosing a form only because an anime character uses it; fictional speech can convey a deliberately exaggerated persona.

04

First Meeting

Put the lesson together

A short first meeting

はじめまして。
アンです。
アメリカ人じんです。
さくら大学だいがくの 学生がくせいです。
どうぞ よろしく お願ねがいします。

Nice to meet you. I am Ann. I am American. I am a student at Sakura University. I look forward to getting to know you.

よろしくお願ねがいします is a set expression used when beginning a relationship or asking for cooperation. It does not have one fixed English translation for every situation.
